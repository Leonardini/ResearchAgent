# Plain, strong writing — the positive target

Removing AI tells is the *negative* goal (what to strip). This is the *positive* goal (what to move
toward). Distilled from **Steve Aaronson, "Style in Scientific Writing"** (1977), which pulls on
Orwell, Zinsser, E.B. White/Strunk, and others.

**The one idea underneath all of it:** the best writing is *transparent* — the reader sees straight
through the words to the meaning. Its enemy is writing-to-impress. Aaronson: *"If a man intends to
impress someone, his work will not be clear, because he does not intend clarity."* That is exactly
what "AI voice" is — abstraction and puffery whose job is to *sound* authoritative. So plain-strong
writing and de-AI'ing are the same task from two sides. Aim for **brevity, clarity, precision.**

**A caution before the rules:** these push toward plainness, but the top-line contract still governs
— preserve every fact, add nothing, keep the formality level. Plainer, not different; plainer, not
emptier.

## Orwell's six rules (the essay endorses them)

1. Never use a metaphor/simile/figure of speech you're used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if there's an everyday equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

## 1. Long/abstract word → short/plain word

The essay's own list (use the plain word unless the longer one is genuinely more precise):

facilitate→ease · numerous→many · remainder→rest · initial→first · implement→do/carry out ·
sufficient→enough · refer to as→call · attempt→try · utilize→use · perform→do · commence→begin ·
cease/desist→stop · conceal→hide · donate→give · visualize→picture · veritable→true · purchase→buy ·
locate→find · obtain→get · transmit→send · proceed→go.

Distrust these dress-up abstractions (Orwell's list) — they exist to add "an air of scientific
impartiality," not meaning: phenomenon, element, individual, objective, categorical, effective,
virtual, basic, primary, promote, constitute, exhibit, utilize. Also: interface, operational, viable,
dimension, replicate, infrastructure, functional, reciprocal, systematized, organizational.

## 2. Kill redundancy and padding

**Redundant doublets** (one word already contains the other) — cut the spare half: personal friend,
short minute, future prospect, solid facts, final conclusion, successful triumph, positive growth,
renovated like new, hopes and aspirations, help and assistance, prompt and speedy, mutual compromise.

**Excess-baggage nouns.** "X of an ADJ character/nature/quality" → just "ADJ X"; and empty
"factor / aspect / condition" usually drops out: "a useful nature"→useful · "the time factor"→the
time · "of an unpleasant character"→unpleasant · "weather conditions"→weather.

**Padding verb phrases** → one verb: render inoperative→break · militate against→hinder · make
contact with→meet · be subjected to→undergo · have the effect of→cause · play a leading part in→lead ·
exhibit a tendency to→tend to · give rise to→cause. **Padding connectives:** with respect to→about ·
the fact that→that · in view of→because · in the interests of→for · on the hypothesis that→if.

## 3. Un-nominalize — turn buried actions back into verbs

This is the fix for **corporate/management-speak**, where every action is frozen into an abstract
noun. Find the buried verb and let it act:

- "streamlined decision-making" → "decides faster" / "made decisions quicker"
- "cross-functional collaboration" → "teams work together across departments"
- "fostered a culture of accountability" → "made people more accountable"
- "operational excellence" → usually empty; cut it, or name the concrete thing it stands for

The claim is preserved; the abstraction is gone. If a nominalization has no real action under it (it's
pure fog), cut it rather than translate it.

## 4. Prefer the active voice

"The wave motions are caused by the wind" → "The wind causes the wave motions." Unnecessary passive
is "tedious, monotonous, lifeless."

**Fidelity guard:** only switch to active when the source names or clearly implies the agent. If the
passive genuinely hides who did it ("mistakes were made", "the sample was contaminated") and the
source never says by whom, do NOT invent an agent to satisfy the rule — that would add a fact. Keep
the passive, or reword without inventing.

## 5. Keep genuine terms of art

Jargon splits in two. Strip the kind used to *dignify* the writer (facilitate, operationalize, robust
synergies, leverage). **Keep** the kind that *denotes* a real, precise concept a plain word can't
replace: leveraged buyout, vector database, p-value, activation energy, REST API. Aaronson grants
this: coined terms are legitimate "when the discovery of a new substance or property demands that new
terms be coined." Precision is part of the target, alongside plainness — never trade an exact term for
a vaguer everyday word.

## 6. Sentence openers: "Notice…" and "And…"

**Neither is an error, and neither should be banned.** Sentence-initial *And* is explicitly sanctioned
by Fowler and by every serious usage guide since; *Notice* is ordinary in mathematical and expository
prose. The note here is about **frequency and function**, not legality. Flagged by the user
2026-08-11, who put it exactly right: not necessarily wrong, but not aligned with what this file is
pushing toward.

Be straight about the provenance: Aaronson's essay does not name either construction. This is an
**extension of its principles**, not a rule quoted from it. The principles it does rest on:

**"Notice that / Notice how / Notice what…"** — this stage-manages the reader. It is an instruction
about *how to read* inserted in front of the content, and it asserts that something is significant
instead of letting the sentence be significant. That is the transparency principle failing (the
reader now sees the writer, not the meaning) and it is a close relative of the **inflated
significance** tell in the main catalogue. It is also nearly always cuttable under Orwell's third
rule, and the sentence hits harder without it:

> "Notice what did not fail. No bad mathematics got through."
> → "No bad mathematics got through."

Same claim, one fewer instruction, more force. Keep *Notice* only where you are genuinely pointing at
something the reader would otherwise miss — a specific feature of an equation, a detail buried in a
quoted passage. If it is pointing at your own argument, cut it.

**Sentence-initial "And"** — the question is whether it is doing **logical** work or **rhythmic**
work. Logical: the sentence really is additive and the conjunction earns its place. Rhythmic: it is
building cadence, a run of short additive sentences that feels like momentum. The second is the tell,
and it is a density phenomenon — one is invisible, three in a paragraph is a drumbeat. When it is
rhythmic, the *And* is a cuttable word (Orwell 3) and cutting it usually costs nothing.

**What actually gives the game away is the cluster.** Machine prose likes to build a paragraph as
*"And X. And Y. Notice Z."* Look for that shape rather than for individual instances, and thin it out
rather than eradicating it — a text with zero sentence-initial conjunctions reads stiff, which is its
own tell. Aim for occasional, not absent.

## The honesty test

When unsure how far to go, ask: *is this word here to carry meaning, or to make the writing (or the
writer) sound more important?* If the latter, it goes. Sincerity, not polish, is what makes writing
read as human.
